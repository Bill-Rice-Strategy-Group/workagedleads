A final expense agent in Indiana wrote to us last month. He is 69 and has been selling life insurance for a long time. He emails his leads, calls them, and texts them. Most calls still go to voicemail, whatever the time or day.

Years ago, he used to reach about 5 people for every 10 calls. Now he sometimes makes 70 to 80 calls in a day and reaches no one.

He wants to work two or three days a week. He is working five and making very little. He asked a fair question: how many times do you call before you give up?

The usual answer is a cadence, such as call six times over two weeks at different hours. That answer misses why the phone stopped working. This article covers the two real causes and the fix: let email decide who you call.

## Why aged leads stopped answering

**Aged leads stop answering for two reasons. Most people now ignore calls from numbers they don't know. And a number that makes a high volume of unanswered calls gets labeled "Spam Likely" by the phone companies, which makes even more people ignore it. More dials at better times make the second problem worse.**

The first cause is a change in habits. In a 2020 survey of more than 10,000 U.S. adults, [Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/12/14/most-americans-dont-answer-cellphone-calls-from-unknown-numbers/) found that about 8 in 10 don't generally answer cellphone calls from unknown numbers. Hiya, a company that screens calls for carriers, surveyed 12,000 consumers in six countries for its [2026 State of the Call report](https://www.hiya.com/state-of-the-call). Only 14% said they immediately answer unknown numbers.

The second cause is the one most agents miss, and it is partly in your control. Three analytics companies decide which calls get labeled for the big carriers: Hiya for AT&T, First Orion for T-Mobile, and TNS. Each one watches how a number behaves.

Our reader's own numbers point to a label. In late October he made 25 calls one morning and had 3 conversations. Two weeks later, 70 to 80 calls a day produced none. People's habits didn't change that much in two weeks. His number's reputation can.

## How to tell if your number is labeled spam

**Call three people you know, one each on Verizon, T-Mobile and AT&T, and ask what their screen showed. If any of them saw "Spam Likely," "Scam Likely" or "Potential Spam," your number is labeled. Register it for free, dispute the label with that carrier, and cut how many calls it makes each day.**

Here is the full check:

1. **Test on all three networks.** A label can appear on one carrier and not the others, because each uses a different analytics company.
2. **Register your number.** The [Free Caller Registry](https://www.freecallerregistry.com/fcr/) is run jointly by First Orion, Hiya and TNS. One form reaches all three. Registration doesn't guarantee your calls will never be labeled, as the registry itself says. It does tell the companies who you are.
3. **Dispute any label you find.** Verizon: [voicespamfeedback.com](https://www.voicespamfeedback.com/vsf/). T-Mobile: [callreporting.t-mobile.com](https://callreporting.t-mobile.com/). AT&T: [att.com/reviewmycalllabel](https://www.att.com/reviewmycalllabel).
4. **Change the behavior that caused it.** Hiya says the main signal is how people react to your calls, meaning blocks and spam reports. The [phone company Dialpad](https://help.dialpad.com/hc/en-us/articles/57941976500251-Reduce-the-Risk-of-Your-Calls-Being-Flagged-as-Spam) lists high volume with low answer rates and sudden jumps in volume among the triggers.

Nobody publishes a safe number of calls per day. Hiya has said that paying a service to "clean" a number misses the point, because the cause is usually the calling pattern. Fewer calls that people expect is how a number gets better. The next section is how you get there.

## Let email decide who you call

**Put every aged lead into a simple email sequence first. Then call only the people who reply, click, or open. Email costs almost nothing per lead, so it can sort a whole batch for you. Your phone time goes to the few who showed interest, and they're more likely to pick up because your name is now familiar.**

This is database marketing. You aren't trying to close anyone by email. You're using email to find out who is still interested, and to make your name familiar before the call.

Our reader emailed his first 58 leads twice and got zero replies. That's normal for two emails, and it isn't a verdict on email. Two emails is a start. The sequence needs to run longer:

- **First month:** a short email every three or four days.
- **After that:** one email a month, for as long as the person stays on your list.

**What to write.** One short question, no pitch, written the way you'd talk. "Did you ever get your burial coverage sorted out?" works. "I will be calling you Tuesday" does not, because it gives the reader nothing to answer. Our [email templates for aged leads](/blog/email-outreach-aged-leads-templates) and [90-day nurture sequence for insurance agents](/blog/aged-lead-nurture-sequences-insurance-agents) have more examples.

**How to read the results.** Treat the signals in this order:

1. **A reply.** Call today. They asked for it.
2. **A click.** Call today. A click means someone read the email and acted.
3. **An open.** Worth a call, but don't lean on it. Apple Mail loads images automatically, so many opens are recorded when nobody actually read the email.
4. **Nothing.** Leave them on the monthly email. They aren't a "no." They aren't ready, or they haven't seen it.

**Which tool.** You need something that sends the sequence by itself and shows who opened and clicked. Don't use Mailchimp or Constant Contact. Both forbid sending to purchased lists in their acceptable-use policies ([Mailchimp](https://mailchimp.com/legal/acceptable_use/), [Constant Contact](https://www.constantcontact.com/legal/acceptable-use-policy)). Insurance CRMs and agent CRMs built on HighLevel run email sequences and calling in one place. Before you sign up, ask for two things: a look at the opens-and-clicks report, and the terms in writing on purchased leads.

Every commercial email has to follow [CAN-SPAM](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business). That means an honest subject line, your physical mailing address, and an unsubscribe link you honor within 10 business days. Any decent CRM handles the address and the link for you.

## How many times should you call an aged lead?

**It depends on what they did with your email. Someone who replied or clicked gets up to six calls over two weeks. Someone who never engaged gets two calls, then goes back on the monthly email. Stop at once for anyone who says no or asks not to be called.**

| What the lead did | Calls | Over |
|---|---|---|
| Replied or clicked | Up to 6 | Two weeks, starting the same day |
| Opened only | 2 to 3 | One week |
| Nothing | 2 | Then monthly email only |
| Said no, or asked not to be called | 0 | Add to your do-not-call list today |

Voicemail on the fifth call doesn't mean "not interested." It usually means they don't know who you are yet. That's the job email does.

For the voicemails you do leave, see [how to leave a voicemail that gets callbacks](/blog/how-to-leave-voicemail-aged-leads).

## Stop texting purchased leads

**Don't text aged leads unless the person has given you written permission. Federal rules treat a text like a call, and marketing texts need the consumer's prior express written consent. The consent on an aged lead usually went to the company that collected it, not to you.**

The FCC's [consumer guide](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/stop-unwanted-robocalls-and-texts) says it plainly: "Commercial texts require written consent." Federal do-not-call rules also apply to texts sent to cellphones ([47 CFR 64.1200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-64/section-64.1200)). Indiana's own law counts a text as a telephone sales call ([IC 24-4.7-2-9](https://iga.in.gov/ic/2026/Title_24/Article_4.7.pdf)).

Indiana's do-not-call law exempts licensed insurance producers soliciting on behalf of an insurer. That exemption covers the state law only. The federal rules still apply to you.

Text the people who tell you it's fine, such as someone who replies "text me." This is guidance, not legal advice. Our article on [whether it's legal to call purchased leads](/blog/is-it-legal-to-call-purchased-leads) covers the rest.

## A two- to three-day week that works

**Load new leads into email on Monday and let the sequence run all week. Spend two or three days calling only the people who replied, clicked or opened. You make fewer calls, to people who know your name, from a number with a clean reputation.**

A sample week:

- **Monday, one hour.** Load new leads into the email sequence. Remove anyone who asked to stop.
- **Tuesday and Thursday.** Call replies and clicks first, then opens, then the second call on new leads. Book appointments.
- **Saturday morning, optional.** Callbacks and appointments.

The email works on the days you don't. That's what makes a shorter week possible.

## What to track

**Track conversations per 100 dials for each phone number, by week. If that number falls sharply while your leads stay the same, check your number for a spam label before you blame the leads.**

Keep four counts each week:

1. Dials
2. Conversations
3. Appointments
4. Which email signal led to each conversation (reply, click, open or none)

After a month you'll know which signals are worth a same-day call. If you track each batch, our [lead cost calculator](/calculators/lead-cost-calculator) turns those counts into cost per conversation.
